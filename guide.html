A note from Dr. Oliver Morris, DO

Your baby is making a sound you've never heard. Or the fever won't come down. Or they've thrown up three times and you can't remember the last wet diaper.

So you do what everyone does. You grab your phone and search. And within a minute you're reading about meningitis, sepsis and a rare syndrome you've never heard of, because search results don't know your baby. They just show you everything.

Now you're more scared than when you started, and you still don't know the one thing you actually need to know: is this nothing, or is this something?

I see the end of that night all the time. I'm an emergency physician. Close to a decade of shifts, and so many of the families who walk through our doors with a baby start with the same sentence: "I'm sorry, I'm probably overreacting."

Usually, they're not overreacting. They just didn't know what to look for. That part can be taught.

I'm also a dad of four, including twins. I've stood over the crib counting breaths too. Knowing the medicine helps. Having it organized so you can use it while exhausted and scared helps a lot more.

That's what this book is. It's the conversation I have at the bedside with worried parents, written down so you have it before you need it.

The three questions ER doctors ask about every child

Before the thermometer, before the test results, these are what we check in the first few seconds. You can check them too.

1

How do they look and act?

Alert, interested and easy to comfort? Or unusually sleepy, floppy, or not themselves?

2

How hard are they breathing?

Easy and comfortable, even if noisy? Or fast and effortful, with skin pulling in at the ribs?

3

Color and fluids?

Normal color and wet diapers? Or pale, gray or blue, with fewer wet diapers than usual?

If all three answers are reassuring, you usually have time to watch and call your pediatrician if things change. If any one of them worries you, get help.
